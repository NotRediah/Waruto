## 5.2.2 Cognitive-Psychological Theories

Cognitive-psychological theories stress the importance of psychological processes involved in foreign policy decision-making, including the effects of historical experiences, personality traits, and leaders’ perceptions and misperceptions that not only affect the definition of the problem, but also the process and outcome of decision-making.

The psychological traits and emotions of decision-makers form the lens through which they tend to define an issue. Their perception of their own capabilities and capacities, as well as the adversary’s intentions and capabilities, influences policy outcomes. Their perception is not merely self-generating and endogenous but is influenced by other actors and factors, including:

- advisors,
- interest and pressure groups,
- structure of the government (democracy or dictatorship),
- continuous inflow of credible or plausible information,
- cognitive capacity to process information,
- historical legacies,
- public opinion, and
- institutional constraints.

Stein outlines four reasons why cognitive-psychological theories may be preferred over rational decision-making theory:

1. Individuals’ preference for simplicity;
2. Consistency, i.e., “people are hard-wired to be conservative” and wary of information which challenges their beliefs, biases, and offers discontinuity;
3. People are poor estimators; and
4. They are more averse to loss than they are disposed to gain-seeking.

Since decision-making is a complex phenomenon involving a multitude of actors and factors, leaders tend to simplify real-world complexities by ignoring specific information which does not satisfy their personal biases. They also do not authenticate the credibility and reliability of the received information. The key concern here is that if the inputs received are selective, the resultant output cannot approximate rationality.

Furthermore, decision-makers simplify decision-making through analogical reasoning, whereby pre-existing frames of reference provide meaning and justification for contemporary cases. President George W. Bush simplified his decision-making by calling Saddam Hussein “the new Hitler” — as was done by Prime Minister Tony Blair who likened Saddam to Hitler — thereby signifying the course of action that was to be adopted in dealing with another Hitler.

Decision-makers usually engage in demonization of belligerents by calling them names to simplify the choices that they must make. President Bush declared Iran, Iraq, and North Korea as part of an “axis of evil.” Although each state in the axis comprises a distinct and independent strategic culture, environment, and dynamics of action/reaction, they were rendered part of a common framework of evilness to simplify the decision-making process.
